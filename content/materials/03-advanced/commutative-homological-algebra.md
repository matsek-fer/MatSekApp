## Pitch

> _AI-generated draft — not yet reviewed by a MatSek member._

Commutative algebra is the study of commutative rings and their modules, and
its reason to exist is geometry: the polynomial functions on a curve or
surface form a ring, and every geometric property of the space — its
dimension, its singular points, how two subvarieties meet — is a property of
that ring. Hilbert's basis theorem, Noether's normalisation, the
Nullstellensatz and primary decomposition were all invented to make that
dictionary work, and in Grothendieck's hands the dictionary became a
definition: a space *is* a ring, read backwards (`geometry`).

Homological algebra is the toolkit that grew out of topology's attempts to
count holes: chain complexes, exact sequences, and the derived functors Ext
and Tor that measure precisely how a construction fails to preserve
exactness. The same machinery counts holes in spaces, classifies extensions
of groups and modules, detects obstructions in geometry, and — in its
categorical form — is the backbone of modern algebraic geometry and
representation theory. It is also the subject where `category-theory-intro`
first becomes indispensable rather than illuminating: derived functors are
defined by a universal property, and nothing shorter works.

The two belong together because the second was built to study the first:
Tor and Ext of modules over a ring are where homological algebra began, and
depth, regularity and smoothness are all homological conditions in disguise.

## Pitch problems

> _AI-generated draft — not yet reviewed by a MatSek member._

1. **Two surfaces that look alike mod 2.** Triangulate the torus and the
   Klein bottle. Define the groups of 0-, 1- and 2-chains with integer
   coefficients and the boundary maps, and compute H₁ = ker ∂₁ / im ∂₂ for
   both. Then repeat with coefficients in ℤ/2. Explain why the two surfaces
   are indistinguishable by the second computation but not by the first, and
   what general principle governs the difference.

   <details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
   The torus has H₁ ≅ ℤ²; the Klein bottle has H₁ ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
   With ℤ/2 coefficients both have H₁ ≅ (ℤ/2)².
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 3</summary>
   The universal coefficient theorem:
   H₁(X; ℤ/2) ≅ H₁(X) ⊗ ℤ/2 ⊕ Tor(H₀(X), ℤ/2). Torsion in H₁ is exactly
   what ℤ/2 coefficients cannot see; Tor is the device that measures it.
   </details>

2. **The ideal of a curve.** Let C ⊂ ℂ³ be the twisted cubic, the set of
   points (t, t², t³). (a) Find polynomials generating the ideal I(C) of all
   polynomials vanishing on C, and prove they generate it. (b) Show that
   I(C) cannot be generated by two polynomials. (c) Prove that *every* ideal
   of ℂ[x, y, z] is finitely generated.

   <details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
   (a) y − x², z − x³ generate; use them to reduce any polynomial to one in
   x alone.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
   (b) Look at the degree-1 part of I(C): is there any linear polynomial in
   it? Count the quadrics.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 3</summary>
   (c) Hilbert's basis theorem, by induction on the number of variables
   using leading coefficients. The non-constructive proof caused a famous
   complaint: "this is not mathematics, it is theology".
   </details>

3. **Counting extensions.** (a) Find, up to isomorphism, all abelian groups
   E containing a subgroup A ≅ ℤ/2 with E/A ≅ ℤ/2. (b) Do the same with
   A ≅ ℤ/m and E/A ≅ ℤ/n, and count how many *different ways* each E arises
   (two ways are the same if there is an isomorphism of E fixing A and
   inducing the identity on E/A). (c) Show that the answer in (b) is the
   group Ext¹(ℤ/n, ℤ/m), and compute it.

   <details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
   (a) ℤ/4 and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
   (b) An extension is determined by where a lift of the generator of ℤ/n
   lands when multiplied by n: an element of ℤ/m modulo n·(ℤ/m).
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 3</summary>
   (c) Ext¹(ℤ/n, ℤ/m) ≅ ℤ/gcd(m, n), computed from the free resolution
   0 → ℤ → ℤ → ℤ/n → 0. Ext classifies extensions; that is where its name
   comes from.
   </details>

## Prerequisites

- **Introduction to algebra** (`algebra-intro`): rings, ideals, quotients,
  modules over a ring, the structure of finitely generated abelian groups.
- **Linear algebra** (`linear-algebra`): exact sequences of vector spaces
  are the model for everything here; Smith normal form for the homology
  computations.
- **Topology** (`topology`): triangulated surfaces, for problem 1.
- **Category theory** (`category-theory-intro`): functors and universal
  properties; derived functors are defined by one.

## From the general materials

- **Napkin** — the parts on modules, the algebraic geometry introduction
  (affine varieties, the Nullstellensatz) and the homological-algebra
  sketches.
- **Princeton Companion** — essays "Commutative algebra", "Homological
  algebra", "Algebraic geometry", "Schemes".
- **Real Not Complex** — Abstract Algebra and Algebraic Geometry pages.
- **nLab** — for the categorical formulation of everything here.

## Materials

### Miles Reid — Undergraduate Commutative Algebra
- **Link:** https://www.cambridge.org/9780521458894
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 3
- **Free:** no
- **Strengths:** The friendly entry: rings, ideals, modules, Noetherian
  rings, the Nullstellensatz, primary decomposition and dimension, with the
  geometric meaning of every theorem stated up front and Reid's opinions
  throughout. Problem 2 is in its first chapters.
- **Weaknesses:** Short; no homological algebra; the humour is not to all
  tastes.

### Atiyah & Macdonald — Introduction to Commutative Algebra
- **Link:** https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.1201/9780429493626/introduction-commutative-algebra-michael-atiyah-ian-macdonald
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 4
- **Free:** no
- **Strengths:** The classic 128-page course: rings and ideals, modules,
  localisation, primary decomposition, integral dependence, Noetherian and
  Artinian rings, dimension theory. Everything needed for `geometry`. The
  exercises are the real content.
- **Weaknesses:** Terse to the point of austerity; no motivation from
  geometry (read alongside Reid or Vakil).

### David Eisenbud — Commutative Algebra with a View Toward Algebraic Geometry
- **Link:** https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-1-4612-5350-1
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 4-5
- **Free:** no (Springer GTM 150)
- **Strengths:** The comprehensive modern text, with the geometry always in
  view and the homological methods (Tor, Ext, depth, regular sequences,
  Koszul complexes) integrated rather than appended. The reference once
  Atiyah–Macdonald runs out.
- **Weaknesses:** 800 pages; not a first course.

### Paolo Aluffi — Algebra: Chapter 0, chapters VIII–IX
- **Link:** https://bookstore.ams.org/gsm-104
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 3-4
- **Free:** no
- **Strengths:** See `algebra-intro`. The last two chapters are the
  gentlest available introduction to homological algebra — complexes,
  homology, derived functors, Ext and Tor — built on the categorical
  language the book has used from the start. Problem 3 is an exercise
  there.
- **Weaknesses:** Assumes the rest of the book.

### Joseph Rotman — An Introduction to Homological Algebra
- **Link:** https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/b98977
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 4
- **Free:** no (Springer Universitext)
- **Strengths:** The readable homological algebra course: modules and
  categories, Hom and ⊗, chain complexes, derived functors, Ext and Tor,
  group and sheaf cohomology, spectral sequences, with far more explanation
  than Weibel and many worked examples. Problem 1's universal coefficient
  theorem is proved in full.
- **Weaknesses:** Long; the second edition has typographical errors.

### Charles Weibel — An Introduction to Homological Algebra
- **Link:** https://www.cambridge.org/9780521559874
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 4-5
- **Free:** no (Cambridge Studies 38)
- **Strengths:** The standard reference: chain complexes, derived functors,
  Tor and Ext, group homology and cohomology, Lie algebra cohomology,
  spectral sequences, derived categories. Everything is here and stated
  precisely.
- **Weaknesses:** Dense; a reference rather than a first exposure.

### Ravi Vakil — The Rising Sea, chapters 1–4
- **Link:** https://math.stanford.edu/~vakil/216blog/
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 4
- **Free:** yes
- **Strengths:** See `geometry`. The opening chapters are the best free
  account of why commutative algebra is geometry and of the categorical and
  homological language (sheaves, exact sequences, Spec) that algebraic
  geometry runs on.
- **Weaknesses:** As there.

## Problems

- **Atiyah & Macdonald** — the exercises are the course; chapters 1–3 and
  6–7 for this topic.
- **Reid** — exercises per chapter with the geometry pointed out.
- **Rotman** — exercises per section; chapters 3 and 6–7 are the drill for
  Ext and Tor.
- **The pitch problems** continue: torus vs Klein bottle → Künneth and the
  universal coefficient theorems in general; the twisted cubic → the
  Nullstellensatz and primary decomposition; extensions → Ext² and the
  classification of extensions of groups by cohomology.
