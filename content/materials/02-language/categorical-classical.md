## Pitch

> _AI-generated draft — not yet reviewed by a MatSek member._

Every field in part 01 was built before category theory existed, and each
carries constructions that look arbitrary until you know what they are for.
Why is the product topology the coarse one? Why does the tensor product have
that strange definition? Why do the quantifiers ∀ and ∃ behave as a matched
pair, and why does ∀x∃y not imply ∃y∀x? Why are p-adic integers, formal
power series and profinite groups so alike?

Seen categorically, each of these has a one-line answer: the construction is
the one with the right universal property, and the universal property is the
whole reason it works. Products in topology, free groups, tensor products,
quotients, completions, quantifiers — all are instances of a handful of
patterns (limits, colimits, adjoints), and once you can see the pattern you
can predict the theorem before proving it.

This topic revisits the fields of part 01 with that lens. It is not more
abstract than the originals — it is shorter. The categorical account of a
field typically deletes the arbitrary choices and keeps the reasons, and the
same half-dozen ideas recur from sets to topology to logic to probability.
It is also the preparation for part 03, where fields are connected to each
other and the categorical language is what makes the connections statable.

## Pitch problems

> _AI-generated draft — not yet reviewed by a MatSek member._

1. **Quantifiers are adjoints.** For a function f : X → Y, image and
   preimage of subsets satisfy f(A) ⊆ B ⟺ A ⊆ f⁻¹(B). Find the operation
   f₊, sending subsets of X to subsets of Y, that satisfies
   f⁻¹(B) ⊆ A ⟺ B ⊆ f₊(A). Interpret f(A) and f₊(A) when f is the
   projection X × Y → X and A is a relation R ⊆ X × Y. Using only these two
   operations, explain why ∀x∃y R(x, y) does not imply ∃y∀x R(x, y) while
   the converse implication holds.

   <details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
   f₊(A) = { y : f⁻¹(y) ⊆ A }.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
   For the projection, the image is "∃y" and f₊ is "∀y".
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 3</summary>
   Quantifiers are adjoints to substitution (Lawvere). The one-way
   implication is an inequality between two composites of adjoints; the
   failure of the other direction is the failure of a Beck–Chevalley
   condition.
   </details>

2. **Which product?** On ℝ^ℕ consider two topologies: (P) the coarsest one
   making every coordinate map continuous; (B) the one generated by all
   boxes ∏ Uₙ with each Uₙ open in ℝ. (a) Show that convergence in (P) is
   coordinatewise convergence, and characterise convergence in (B).
   (b) Show that [0, 1]^ℕ is compact in (P) but not in (B). (c) Which of the
   two deserves to be called *the* product — for a reason that does not
   mention compactness?

   <details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
   In (B), a box around a point can exclude every sequence that differs
   from it in infinitely many coordinates.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
   Tychonoff for (P). For (B), find an infinite set with no accumulation
   point.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 3</summary>
   (P) is the categorical product: continuous maps into it are exactly
   families of continuous maps into the factors. (B) fails this. The
   universal property is the reason; compactness is the reward.
   </details>

3. **The limit of the clocks.** Fix a prime p. A *compatible sequence* is
   (a₁, a₂, …) with aₙ ∈ ℤ/pⁿ and aₙ₊₁ ≡ aₙ (mod pⁿ). (a) Show that the
   compatible sequences form a ring ℤ_p containing ℤ, in which every element
   not divisible by p is invertible. (b) Show that in ℤ₇ the equation x² = 2
   has a solution, and compute a₁, a₂, a₃. (c) Show that ℤ_p is uncountable,
   and that a ring homomorphism from any ring R into ℤ_p is the same thing as
   a compatible family of homomorphisms R → ℤ/pⁿ.

   <details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
   (a) Invert modulo each pⁿ; check the inverses are compatible.
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
   (b) Lift a solution modulo 7ⁱ to one modulo 7ⁱ⁺¹ (Hensel's lemma).
   </details>
   <details><summary>Hint 3</summary>
   (c) ℤ_p is the limit of the diagram of clocks. The same universal
   property defines power series rings and profinite groups, which is why
   all three behave alike.
   </details>

  adjunctions, Yoneda.

  and ideally `real-analysis` and `probability`. The point of the topic is
  the comparison, so the classical version should be known first.

## Materials

### Bradley, Bryson & Terilla — Topology: A Categorical Approach
- **Link:** https://topology.mitpress.mit.edu/
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 3
- **Free:** yes (MIT Press open access)
- **Strengths:** Point-set topology rebuilt around universal properties:
  the product, quotient and subspace topologies as the categorical
  constructions they are, compactness and connectedness via functors, the
  fundamental group as a functor and Van Kampen as a pushout. Short, and
  the best single demonstration of what this topic claims. Problem 2 lives
  here.
- **Weaknesses:** Assumes a first topology course; the categorical
  prerequisites are introduced quickly.

### Paolo Aluffi — Algebra: Chapter 0
- **Link:** https://bookstore.ams.org/gsm-104
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 3-4
- **Free:** no
- **Strengths:** See `algebra-intro`. Listed here because it is the
  algebra half of this topic: groups, rings, modules and fields developed
  with universal properties from the first chapter, and homological algebra
  at the end.
- **Weaknesses:** Long; a reader who knows algebra will want to skim the
  early chapters for the viewpoint only.

### Tom Leinster — Rethinking Set Theory
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.6543
- **Type:** paper
- **Difficulty:** 2-3
- **Free:** yes
- **Strengths:** Ten axioms about sets and functions (Lawvere's ETCS) that
  suffice for all ordinary mathematics, presented in twelve pages for a
  reader who has never seen category theory. Shows that "set theory seen
  categorically" is simpler, not harder, than ZFC.
- **Weaknesses:** Deliberately introductory; the comparison with ZFC
  (replacement, the role of elements) is only sketched.

### Robert Goldblatt — Topoi: The Categorial Analysis of Logic
- **Link:** https://projecteuclid.org/ebooks/books-by-independent-authors/Topoi-The-Categorial-Analysis-of-Logic/toc/bia/1403013939
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 3-4
- **Free:** yes (Project Euclid; Dover in print)
- **Strengths:** Logic seen categorically, from scratch: categories,
  subobjects, the subobject classifier, topoi as universes of sets,
  internal logic (intuitionistic by default), quantifiers as adjoints,
  with set theory and the continuum hypothesis revisited at the end. Slow
  and very readable. Problem 1 is chapter 15.
- **Weaknesses:** 1979; notation is dated and the later literature is
  absent; long.

### F. W. Lawvere — Metric Spaces, Generalized Logic and Closed Categories
- **Link:** http://www.tac.mta.ca/tac/reprints/articles/1/tr1abs.html
- **Type:** paper
- **Difficulty:** 4
- **Free:** yes (TAC reprint with author's commentary)
- **Strengths:** The paper that showed a metric space is a category
  enriched in [0, ∞]: distances are hom-objects, the triangle inequality is
  composition, Cauchy completeness is a Yoneda statement. Analysis seen
  categorically, and the origin of a whole branch of the subject.
- **Weaknesses:** Dense and allusive; requires enriched categories, which
  are not in Leinster or Riehl.

### Tobias Fritz — A synthetic approach to Markov kernels …
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07021
- **Type:** paper
- **Difficulty:** 4
- **Free:** yes
- **Strengths:** See `applied-category-theory`. Listed here as the
  probability half of this topic: probability theory from the axioms of a
  Markov category.
- **Weaknesses:** As there.

### Emily Riehl — Category Theory in Context
- **Link:** https://emilyriehl.github.io/books/
- **Type:** book
- **Difficulty:** 3-4
- **Free:** yes
- **Strengths:** See `category-theory-intro`. Listed here because its
  method is this topic's method: every concept is illustrated by what it
  does in topology, algebra, analysis and logic, so it serves as the
  connecting text for all the field-specific materials above.
- **Weaknesses:** Organised by categorical concept, not by field.
